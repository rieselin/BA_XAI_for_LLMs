This chapter presents the findings of the user study conducted to evaluate how different
explainability methods affect user trust in large language model (LLM) outputs. The study
compared five conditions: a plain LLM baseline, Chain-of-Thought reasoning (CoT), SHAP
feature attributions, confidence scores (CONF), and an explanation on the working of an LLM.
Trust was measured across five Likert-scaled items (1\,=\,strongly disagree,
5\,=\,strongly agree). The following sections first characterize the participant sample,
then report trust outcomes per method, and finally describe the relative helpfulness
rankings provided by participants.

\section{Sample Characteristics}
\label{sec:sample}
 
\subsection{Participant Overview}
 
The survey was completed by a total sample from which \textbf{175 participants} confirmed
prior use of at least one LLM. Only these participants were routed to the main study
questions, including the trust ratings for the five explanation conditions and the
helpfulness rankings. All analyses reported in Sections~\ref{sec:trust} and
\ref{sec:rankings} are therefore based on this subset of $n = 175$.
 
The panel was designed to be \textbf{equally distributed} across six age groups
(18--24, 25--34, 35--44, 45--54, 55--64, and 65+ years) and \textbf{balanced by
gender} (female vs.\ male/other), resulting in a quota-sampled design intended to ensure
broad demographic coverage rather than convenience-based recruitment. Each age--gender
cell was thus approximately equally represented, limiting age or gender confounds in
the interpretation of trust outcomes.
 
\subsection{Familiarity and Prior Use of Generative AI}
 
Of all survey respondents, \textbf{67.3\%} reported being familiar with generative AI
tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Claude, LLaMA, or Bard (see
Figure~\ref{fig:familiar}). Of those familiar with GenAI \textbf{77.4\%} indicated having
used at least one such tool at least once (Figure~\ref{fig:used}). Participants who had
never used an LLM were asked for their reasons but did not proceed to the main study;
among them, the most frequently cited barrier was \emph{not yet having seen a need}
($n=37$), followed by a preference for traditional search engines ($n=21$) and privacy or
security concerns ($n=17$) (Figure~\ref{fig:nonusers}).

\subsection{Employment Role and Sector}
\label{sec:sample_demographics}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:role} shows LLM usage broken down by employment role. Administration
($n=26$), Retired ($n=21$), and Specialist/Expert ($n=19$) were the three most represented
groups, while technical roles such as IT/Software/Data ($n=15$) and Technical
Staff/Engineer ($n=11$) together comprised a moderate share of users. The broad spread
across non-technical roles suggests that the findings are not exclusively representative
of a technically literate audience.

\section{Trust Across Explanation Methods}
\label{sec:trust}
 
\subsection{Overall Trust Distributions}
\label{sec:trust_dist}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot_method} presents aggregated trust distributions for each of the
five methods. The plain LLM baseline achieved the highest median trust (Mdn\,=\,4.0) and
the tightest interquartile range, suggesting that in the absence of any explanation,
participants defaulted to moderate-to-high trust in the system's outputs. The Chain of Thought (CoT) produced the \emph{lowest} median trust score (Mdn\,=\,3.0) across all methods, indicating that
exposing the model's reasoning process frequently caused participants to revise their trust
\emph{downward}. SHAP (Mdn\,=\,3.4) and the "How an LLM works" explanation (EXPL) (Mdn\,=\,3.6) occupied intermediate positions, while the confidence score (CONF) (Mdn\,=\,3.0) also remained below the LLM baseline.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{diagrams/survey_diagrams/trust_per_method_dist.png}
  \caption{Distribution of trust levels per explanation method (all five trust aspects
           combined). Orange lines indicate medians; circles indicate outliers.}
  \label{fig:boxplot_method}
\end{figure}
 
\subsection{Trust by Aspect and Method}
\label{sec:heatmap}
 
The heat-map in Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} disaggregates trust into the five individual
aspects across methods. Three patterns stand out:
 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The LLM baseline consistently scores highest.} Mean ratings range from
        3.5 (``I would rely on the information'') to 4.1 (``I believe the system has
        tried to provide accurate answers'').
  \item \textbf{CoT consistently scores lowest.} Mean ratings across aspects range from
        2.7 to 3.5, and every aspect is rated lower under CoT than under all other
        methods except CONF.
  \item \textbf{EXPL outperforms SHAP and CONF on most aspects.} Particularly for
        perceived competence (3.9 vs.\ 3.4 for SHAP) and for reliability (3.7 vs.\ 3.3).
  \item \textbf{Participants have a consistent high trust that ``I believe the system has
        tried to provide accurate answers''} which is not something a generative LLM does.
\end{enumerate}
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{diagrams/survey_diagrams/changeOfTrust_heat.png}
  \caption{Mean trust ratings per method and aspect (scale 1--5). Warmer colours
           indicate higher trust.}
  \label{fig:heatmap}
\end{figure}
 
The line chart in Figure~\ref{fig:lineplot} confirms that the trust trajectory dips
sharply at CoT for every aspect, recovers partially at SHAP, then narrows again at CONF
before recovering at EXPL. The gap between the \emph{best-performing aspect} (``I believe the system has tried to provide accurate answers'') and the worst (``I would rely on the
information'') is consistent across all methods, suggesting that \emph{intention
attribution} is persistently rated more charitably than \emph{practical reliance}.

\subsection{Individual Trajectories}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:individual} plots per-participant trust trajectories across methods.
Although considerable individual heterogeneity is visible, the majority of lines converge
around levels 3 and 4, and the dip at CoT is apparent even at the individual level.
Participants who began with high trust under LLM (level\,5) showed the sharpest drops at
CoT, while those who started at level\,3 or below were less affected. This suggests a
\emph{prior-expectation effect}: CoT disrupts the trust of initially confident users more
than that of sceptical ones.
 
\subsection{Trust Variation by Number of LLMs Used}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:violin} shows violin plots of trust per aspect, grouped by the number of different
LLMs a participant had previously used. No clear monotonic relationship is evident;
participants who had used six or more LLMs showed greater variance rather than
systematically higher or lower trust, possibly because heavier users have formed more
nuanced, context-dependent judgments. Median trust remained consistently near 4.0 across
experience levels for most aspects, suggesting that prior LLM exposure does not
substantially moderate the effect of explanation type on trust.

\section{Perceived Helpfulness Rankings of XAI Methods}
\label{sec:rankings}
 
The 50\% of the participants that viewed all three XAI methods (SHAP, CoT, Confidence), were asked to
rank them from most to least helpful. Figure~\ref{fig:ranking} shows the distribution of rank positions.
 
\textbf{Chain-of-Thought} received the most ``Ranked \#1'' votes ($n = 45$), making it the most frequently preferred explanation type despite producing the \emph{lowest} mean trust scores. \textbf{Confidence scores} were ranked first by a comparable number of participants ($n = 43$) and accumulated the most second-place votes. \textbf{SHAP} was ranked first by the fewest participants ($n = 27$) and collected the most third-place votes, indicating it was perceived as the least helpful of the three explanations.
 
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{diagrams/survey_diagrams/ranked_xai_methods.png}
    \caption{Stacked bar chart of helpfulness rankings for the three XAI methods. Each
           participant ranked SHAP, CoT, and Confidence from most (1) to least (3)
           helpful.}
    \label{fig:ranking}
\end{figure}
 
The juxtaposition of low trust with high perceived helpfulness for CoT is noteworthy.

It suggests that participants found CoT \emph{informative}, it exposed reasoning steps they could evaluate, but that this very transparency revealed uncertainty or errors that reduced their trust in the answers. This pattern aligns with the concept of \emph{appropriate calibration} in human-centred XAI: an explanation that lowers inflated trust may still be considered useful if it aids decision-making \cite{zhang_effect_2020}.

CoT can therefore be characterized as a \emph{high-information, trust-calibrating} explanation type, whereas confidence scores appear to function as a more \emph{trust-preserving} but lower-information signal.
 
SHAP's low helpfulness ranking, despite producing intermediate trust scores, is consistent with prior literature suggesting that feature-importance visualizations are poorly understood by non-technical audiences \cite{chromik_i_2021, kaur_interpreting_2020}. Given the demographic composition of this sample (dominated by administrative, healthcare, and retired participants) the relatively technical nature of SHAP values likely contributed to its unfavourable reception.
