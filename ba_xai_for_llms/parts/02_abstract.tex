Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini have become embedded
in the daily routines of a broad and diverse population, the majority of whom are not AI
specialists. Yet these systems remain susceptible to hallucination and dishonesty, delivering
confident outputs regardless of their accuracy. Users who cannot distinguish a correct answer
from a fluent but incorrect one risk either placing trust where it is not warranted or, following
negative experiences, developing excessive skepticism that prevents them from benefiting from
the technology's genuine capabilities. Explainable AI (XAI) has emerged as the principal
mechanism for addressing this challenge, but empirical evidence on which explanation types
are most effective for lay users remains sparse.

This thesis investigates the impact of three XAI methods: Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting,
SHAP-based token attribution combined with counterfactual reasoning, and confidence
indication on user trust in LLM outputs. A functional web-based demonstrator running on
Llama 3.1 was built to generate explanation stimuli across three use case categories: legal,
medical, and general factual queries. An empirical user study with $n = 175$ prior LLM users
measured trust across five dimensions before and after exposure to each explanation method,
using a repeated-measures design with randomization into single-method and all-methods
conditions.

The results show that all three XAI methods reduced trust relative to a plain LLM baseline,
but that this reduction reflects appropriate recalibration rather than failure. Chain-of-Thought
prompting produced the lowest median trust scores of all conditions while simultaneously
receiving the highest perceived helpfulness rankings, suggesting that its transparency exposes
errors and inconsistencies that appropriately reduce inflated trust. Confidence indication
achieved a comparable helpfulness ranking with lower information overhead, functioning as an
efficient trust-preserving signal. SHAP attribution, despite producing intermediate trust scores,
was ranked least helpful by the majority of participants, consistent with prior literature on
the inaccessibility of feature-importance visualizations to non-technical audiences. A general
explanation of how LLMs work produced competitive trust outcomes across all aspects,
underscoring the value of conceptual framing alongside per-output explanation methods.

Building on these findings, a prototype was developed that combines Chain-of-Thought
reasoning with per-step confidence scores and automatic regeneration of low-confidence steps.
This design targets the central tension identified in the data: making the model's reasoning
transparent while equipping lay users with a simple, immediately interpretable signal for
calibrating reliance on individual steps. The thesis concludes with concrete design
recommendations for trustworthy LLM-based applications and identifies directions for future
research, including the challenge of making technically faithful explanation methods accessible
to non-specialist users.
