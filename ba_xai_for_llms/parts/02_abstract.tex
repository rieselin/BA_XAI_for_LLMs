Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini have become embedded in the daily routines of a broad and diverse population, most of whom are not AI specialists. Yet these systems remain susceptible to hallucination and dishonesty, delivering confident outputs regardless of accuracy, which leaves users poorly equipped to judge when to trust them. Explainable AI (XAI) has emerged as the principal mechanism for addressing this challenge, but empirical evidence on which explanation types best support trust calibration in lay users remains sparse.

This thesis investigates three XAI methods (Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, SHAP-based token attribution with counterfactual reasoning, and confidence indication) and their effect on user trust in LLM outputs. A functional web-based demonstrator running on Llama 3.1 was built to generate explanation stimuli across legal, medical, and general factual queries. An empirical user study with 175 qualifying prior LLM users measured trust across five aspects before and after exposure to each method, using a repeated-measures design with randomization into single-method and all-methods conditions.

All three methods reduced trust relative to a plain LLM baseline, reflecting appropriate recalibration rather than failure. CoT received the highest perceived helpfulness rankings while simultaneously producing the lowest median trust scores, suggesting that its transparency exposes errors that correctly deflate inflated trust. Confidence indication achieved comparable helpfulness with lower information overhead. SHAP produced intermediate trust scores but was ranked least helpful, consistent with prior literature on the inaccessibility of feature-importance visualizations to non-technical audiences. A general explanation of how LLMs work produced competitive trust outcomes across all aspects, underscoring the value of conceptual framing alongside per-output methods.

A prototype combining CoT with per-step confidence scores and automatic regeneration of low-confidence steps is presented, targeting the central tension between reasoning transparency and lay interpretability. The thesis concludes with design recommendations for trustworthy LLM-based applications and directions for future research.
